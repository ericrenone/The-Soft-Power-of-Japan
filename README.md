## 1. Two Scenes

**Tokyo, April 1, 2026.** The President of France and the Prime Minister of Japan have just finished a press conference about a ceasefire in the Middle East, safe passage through the Strait of Hormuz, a fast-breeder reactor, and a program for recycling nuclear fuel. They shake hands. Then Sanae Takaichi draws her cupped palms back to her hip and thrusts them forward, and Emmanuel Macron, without a second's hesitation, laughs and does the same. It is the Kamehameha, the signature attack of a cartoon boy named Goku. Macron posts the clip within hours. His caption is one word, in Japanese: *Fusion!*

**Los Angeles, March 23, 2003.** The Academy Awards are about to give *Spirited Away* the Oscar for Best Animated Feature, the first Japanese film ever to win it. The director, Hayao Miyazaki, is not in the room. He has refused to fly to the United States because the United States is bombing Iraq, a reason he keeps to himself for six years at his producer's request, until he finally says it out loud in 2009: he did not want to visit a country that was bombing Iraq.

Here is the puzzle these two scenes make together. Japan's two most powerful cultural exports of the last forty years were made by men who could not be more different in how they treated their work and the world. Akira Toriyama drew a boy so simple a child could copy him, said repeatedly that he never wanted to be a cartoonist, hid behind a robot avatar, and let his shapes go wherever they went. Miyazaki draws every frame, forbids a single cut, publicly calls his own prime minister's constitutional plans despicable, and once boycotted the most important award in his industry over a war. One built an empty vessel that any state could fill. The other built a full one that no state can touch.

Both of them became Japanese soft power. The question is how, and the answer turns out to be the whole history of how Japan went from a defeated empire to a country whose cartoons are performed by presidents. It is a story in which the government is almost always the last to arrive.

Let's follow it from the beginning, the way you'd trace a dish on your plate back through every hand that touched it.

## 2. What "Soft Power" Means, and Why Japan Is the Strange Case

An American political scientist named Joseph Nye coined the term in 1990. Hard power is making others do what you want with force or money. Soft power is getting them to want what you want, through attraction: your culture, your values, your example. Nye was writing about the United States, and in the American version the state is usually in the picture, funding exchange programs, broadcasting, Hollywood as an informal ambassador.

Japan is the strange case, and it is strange in three ways.

First, Japan acquired enormous soft power without trying. For most of the period in which its cartoons conquered foreign television, the Japanese government had no policy about cartoons at all, and was mildly embarrassed by them.

Second, when the state finally did try, it mostly failed. The flagship vehicle, the Cool Japan Fund, launched in 2013, reported cumulative losses of 54 billion yen in fiscal 2025 against a target of 42.6 billion, and in August 2026 the industry ministry decided to effectively abolish it by submitting a budget without money for it. The state could not originate what the market had produced for free.

Third, and this is the part that makes the story worth telling, the state eventually learned to do something cheaper and better than originate. It learned to perform. The Kamehameha at the palace cost nothing and reached more people than the fund ever did.

To understand how Japan got from the first stage to the third, you have to understand that soft power is not a thing a country has. It is a thing that gets grown, by someone, somewhere, under some constraint, and then travels through channels nobody designed. Every ingredient has a farm. So let's find the farms.

## 3. Part One: The Ingredient Loop (1958–1984)

### A boy sneaks out to the movies

In 1958, a Tokyo high-schooler who should have been studying for his entrance exams snuck out to see *The White Snake Enchantress*, Japan's first feature-length color cartoon. He fell in love with the heroine and cried, and later wrote that the film pushed him toward making work true to his own feelings rather than imitating what was popular. The boy was Hayao Miyazaki. The film was made by Toei, which had set itself up to be the Disney of the East, and the story it told was Chinese: the Legend of the White Snake, a folk tale from Hangzhou.

Hold onto that detail. The first thing that made Japan's greatest animator want to animate was a Japanese studio imitating an American studio to tell a Chinese story. The loop that would define Japanese soft power was already running, and no one had named it.

### Heidi, made in Tokyo, sold to the Alps

Miyazaki joined Toei in 1963 as an in-betweener on 19,500 yen a month. There he met Isao Takahata, a University of Tokyo graduate in French literature who had gotten into animation because of a French film, *The King and the Mockingbird*, and who would become his lifelong collaborator. The two of them were union officers, Miyazaki chief secretary and Takahata vice-chairman, and they left Toei together in 1971, dissatisfied with management.

What they did next is the part of Japanese soft power that almost nobody includes in the story. Between 1974 and 1979, at a studio that became Nippon Animation, Takahata directed and Miyazaki laid out three long television adaptations of European children's novels: *Heidi, Girl of the Alps*, *3000 Leagues in Search of Mother*, and *Anne of Green Gables*. They flew to Switzerland and sketched the mountains. They went to Argentina and Italy. They went to Prince Edward Island. Takahata gave *Heidi* a realistic style that showed the seasons changing, the rural work, the cooking, the small details of Alpine life.

Then the shows were sold to Europe and Latin America, and in Spain, Italy, Germany and across the Spanish-speaking world, *Heidi* became one of the most beloved children's programs ever broadcast. Japan had taken a Swiss novel, rebuilt it with Japanese craftsmanship, and sold it back to the Swiss, the Germans, and the Spanish, who received it as theirs.

This is the ingredient loop. Japan's cultural exports have, from the beginning, been imports refined and returned. Toriyama's Goku is a Chinese monkey run through Hong Kong cinema. Miyazaki's heroines are Nausicaä from Homer and Heidi from Spyri. Takahata's *Anne* is Canadian. Steve Jobs, as the companion essays show, took Zen and Braun and a monk's calligraphy and sent them back as the Macintosh. The thing that crossed the Pacific was never a style. It was a standard of care applied to someone else's story.

### The hardware decade, and the fear

Meanwhile a different Japan was conquering the world with hardware. The Walkman in 1979. The Famicom in 1983. By the late 1980s, American bookstores carried titles like *Japan as Number One* and American politicians smashed Toshiba radios on the Capitol lawn. When Sony bought Columbia Pictures in 1989, the cover of a national magazine showed the Statue of Liberty in a kimono. This was hard power dressed as commerce, and it provoked exactly the backlash hard power provokes.

But underneath the hardware, something softer was traveling, and it was traveling because of the hardware. The Famicom carried *Dragon Quest*, and *Dragon Quest* carried a teardrop with two dots and a smile drawn by a reluctant cartoonist from Nagoya. The cassette decks and the televisions were the pipes. Nobody was watching what flowed through them.

## 4. Part Two: The Two Founders of 1984 (1984–1989)

### The same year, twice

The companion essay on 1985 tells the story of Jobs and Toriyama reaching the same fork at thirty. The soft-power story has a different pair reaching a different fork in the same year, and this time they are both Japanese.

In March 1984, *Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind* opened in Japanese cinemas, directed by Miyazaki, produced by Takahata, scored by a minimalist composer named Joe Hisaishi whom Takahata had found. It was a hit. In November 1984, *Dragon Ball* began in *Weekly Shōnen Jump*, chosen week after week by children's survey cards. On June 15, 1985, Miyazaki and Takahata, with Toshio Suzuki and the publisher Tokuma Shoten, founded Studio Ghibli, named for a hot desert wind and an Italian scout plane, to blow something new through a stagnant industry.

Two engines of Japanese soft power were switched on within fifteen months of each other. And in the same window, something happened that would set the two engines on opposite tracks for the next forty years.

### Warriors of the Wind

In June 1985, a company called New World Pictures released *Nausicaä* in the United States under the title *Warriors of the Wind*. They had cut about twenty-two minutes. They renamed the heroine Princess Zandra. They removed the environmental themes, simplified the plot, and put men on the poster who were not in the film. Miyazaki and Takahata were so disgusted that they refused to consider Western releases of their films for a decade, and Studio Ghibli adopted a strict no-cuts policy for all foreign licensing that it keeps to this day.

Now compare what happened to *Dragon Ball* when it went abroad. In France, *Club Dorothée* aired it from 1988 with a French dub. In Latin America, Mexican studios dubbed it in the mid-1990s. In the United States, Funimation re-cut episodes, rewrote dialogue, and replaced the entire score with new music by a composer named Nathan Johnson, who later became a Texas state senator. Toriyama, by every account, did not object. He had designed a thing that survived mistreatment. The companion essays call this the compression instinct: strip the object to the fewest marks that carry its identity, so that it survives any pipe. Goku survived the Funimation pipe. *Nausicaä* did not survive New World's.

So here is the first fork. Studio Ghibli chose fidelity: the object travels whole or it does not travel. Toriyama's work chose compression: the object travels in whatever form the receiver wants, because its identity lives in a silhouette no editor can cut. These are two export protocols, and Japanese soft power needs both. Fidelity gets you Cannes and the Oscars. Compression gets you the Kamehameha at the palace.

### A cat-bus and a double bill

The second thing that happened in Ghibli's early years is the reason the studio survived to make the fidelity strategy work, and it is almost comically close to the Dragon Quest Slime story.

In 1988 Ghibli released *My Neighbor Totoro* on a double bill with Takahata's *Grave of the Fireflies*. Suzuki had engineered the pairing: *Grave*, based on a famous short story about two children starving in the last months of the war, had educational value, so schools would send students, and they would see *Totoro* too. The box office was modest. The studio was fragile. Then, in 1990, Ghibli approved merchandising for Totoro, the giant forest spirit with a round body, two eyes, and a wide mouth, and the merchandise profits alone sustained the studio for years.

Totoro is Ghibli's Slime. He is the one empty face in a catalogue of full ones: a closed contour, minimal features, no plot required, equally legible at five inches or fifty feet. He became the studio's mascot for the same reason the Slime became Dragon Quest's: he is the one element that doesn't change when the cast does. An asteroid is named after him. Akira Kurosawa put the film among his hundred favorites. And the studio that refused every cut to its films was kept alive by a plush toy.

The lesson is uncomfortable and true: even the fidelity strategy needs one compressed shape to pay the bills.

### The road not taken

One more thing from these years, because it is the shadow of everything that follows. In April 1989, Takahata published an outline for a film called *Border 1939*, about a Japanese student traveling through occupied Korea, Manchuria, China and Mongolia at the start of the Pacific War. Where *Grave of the Fireflies* showed Japanese children as victims at the war's end, *Border 1939* would have shown Japanese aggression at its start. Takahata wanted to confront young viewers with their country's imperialism directly. Then, after the Tiananmen crackdown that June, Japanese attitudes toward China hardened, the distributor judged a Chinese setting too risky, and the project died before a single frame was drawn.

Takahata himself rejected the anti-war reading of *Grave of the Fireflies*. He saw Seita's tragedy as a story about pride and isolation, not about war. But the film the world received is a film about Japanese suffering, and the film Japan did not make is a film about Japanese guilt. Soft power is also made of what gets left out.

## 5. Part Three: The Lost Decade and the Accidental Empire (1990–2002)

### The economy dies; the cartoons take off

In 1990 the Japanese asset bubble burst. Stocks fell by more than half. Land prices collapsed. The 1990s became the Lost Decade, and then the lost two decades, and Japan went from the country that was going to buy America to the country that could not grow.

And in exactly those years, Japanese popular culture conquered the planet. *Dragon Ball Z* premiered in 1989 and reached Latin America and France in the early-to-mid 1990s. *Sailor Moon* went global. *Pokémon* launched in 1996 and hit the United States in 1998, and within a year American children could name a hundred and fifty Japanese-designed monsters. Tamagotchi. Hello Kitty. By 1995, *Weekly Shōnen Jump* was selling 6.53 million copies a week, a record, and *Dragon Ball* ended that year at 519 chapters because its author was exhausted.

Nobody in the Japanese government planned any of this. The hardware had made the pipes, the Lost Decade had made Japan seem harmless rather than threatening, and the children of forty countries were watching.

### Disney distributes Japan

In July 1996, after four years of negotiation, the Walt Disney Company signed a deal with Tokuma Shoten to be the worldwide distributor of Studio Ghibli's films, and to put up ten percent of the budget for future ones. Miyazaki approved it, he said, not for the money but to support the publisher that had supported him.

Read that sentence slowly. The company that had defined what animation was, the one Miyazaki had accused in 1988 of showing contempt for its audience, was now the pipe through which Miyazaki's films would reach the world. And it was a pipe with a condition attached, the no-cuts policy. When Miramax wanted to trim *Princess Mononoke* in 1999 for a lower rating, Ghibli refused, and the film grossed about $2.3 million in American theaters, a failure, and was also the moment the studio became known in the West. Neil Gaiman wrote the English script. Fidelity costs reach. Ghibli paid.

### Spirited Away, and the word arrives

In July 2001 *Spirited Away* opened in Japan and sold 21.4 million tickets, the most in Japanese history, a record it held for nearly twenty years. In February 2002 it won the Golden Bear at Berlin, the first animated film to do so. In March 2003 it won the Oscar, and the director stayed home.

And in 2002, between those two prizes, an American journalist named Douglas McGray published an essay in *Foreign Policy* called "Japan's Gross National Cool." It argued that while Japan's economy had stagnated, its cultural influence, from Pokémon to Hello Kitty to anime, had become a kind of national power no one was measuring. The essay became the single most-cited piece on the subject and the seed of every policy that followed.

Notice the order of events. The children of France had been watching Goku for fourteen years. Totoro had been paying Ghibli's bills for twelve. Pokémon had been on American lunchboxes for four. Then an American wrote an essay, and Japan learned it had soft power. The word came after the thing, and the thing had been grown by publishers, broadcasters, toy companies and two cartoonists who did not want to be famous.

## 6. Part Four: The State Wakes Up (2003–2013)

### A soccer comic on a water truck

The Japanese state's first documented use of a comic as a diplomatic tool was not a cultural program. It was a logistics problem.

In 2004, Japan's Self-Defense Forces deployed to Samawah in southern Iraq, their first overseas deployment in a combat zone since 1945, and they needed Iraqis not to attack their water trucks. Someone realized that Iraqi children knew a Japanese soccer cartoon called *Captain Tsubasa*, broadcast across the Arab world as *Captain Majid* in the 1990s. Stickers of Tsubasa went on the trucks. The trucks were left alone.

That was the whole theory of Japanese soft power, demonstrated in the desert: a comic from the same magazine as *Dragon Ball*, chosen by Japanese children's survey cards in 1981, dubbed into Arabic by someone in the Gulf, watched by Iraqi ten-year-olds in 1995, and recognized instantly by those children, now teenagers, in 2004. The state did not make the comic, did not dub it, did not broadcast it. It only noticed, twenty-three years late, that it could ride it.

### Manga diplomacy

The noticing became policy under Taro Aso, a foreign minister and later prime minister who read manga himself and said so. In 2006 he gave a speech on cultural diplomacy at a university devoted to digital content. In 2007 the Foreign Ministry created the International Manga Award. In March 2008 it appointed Doraemon, the blue robot cat, as Japan's first "anime ambassador," and the same year Hello Kitty became a tourism ambassador to China and Hong Kong. In 2009 three young women were sent abroad as "kawaii ambassadors" in Lolita fashion and school uniforms. In 2010 the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry opened a Cool Japan office, and in 2013, under Shinzo Abe, the Cool Japan Fund was capitalized to invest in exporting Japanese culture.

The theory behind all this was the opposite of what had actually happened. The fund assumed soft power could be originated from Tokyo with capital. The record said soft power had been originated from Tokyo by a magazine with a survey, and distributed from Paris, Mexico City and Dallas by broadcasters who paid for it. The fund invested 204 billion yen in 83 projects. Its cumulative losses reached 54 billion yen. In 2026 it was wound down.

The companion essays have a name for why. The thing that chose Goku was a *numbered no*, a weekly ranking by children that no editor could overrule. The fund had a committee. Committees do not have numbers; they have opinions. The state could ratify what the survey had chosen, as it did with Doraemon and Tsubasa. It could not replace the survey.

### Honoring the man who attacks you

While the state was building Cool Japan, it was also honoring Hayao Miyazaki, and the two things do not sit easily together.

In November 2012 the Japanese government named Miyazaki a Person of Cultural Merit. In July 2013 *The Wind Rises* opened, a film about the designer of the Zero fighter, and within weeks Miyazaki publicly attacked Prime Minister Abe's push to revise the pacifist constitution, calling the attempt to leave his name in history by revising it despicable, and saying people who don't think enough shouldn't touch the constitution. In 2015 he said Japan should clearly state that it inflicted enormous damage on China and apologize properly to the comfort women. He suggested the disputed Senkaku Islands be split in half.

So the state's single most prestigious living cultural export was, on the record, a man who thought the state was wrong about the war, wrong about the constitution, wrong about China, and wrong about Korea. He had already refused to attend the Oscars over Iraq. He considers himself a leftist by emotional affinity. He labels his own films *manga eiga*, cartoon films, specifically to avoid the word "anime" and the subculture it carries, and he has said the anime industry is full of people who cannot stand looking at other people.

The state's solution was elegant and is the key to the whole Ghibli half of this story: **honor the object, mute the author.** Give the Person of Cultural Merit to the man, put *Spirited Away* on the tourism posters, and never quote him. Nobody can perform a Miyazaki film the way they can perform a Kamehameha. The films are full; there is no empty space for a prime minister to step into. So Miyazaki became soft power as an institution, Studio Ghibli, a museum in Mitaka from 2001, a theme park in Aichi from 2022, honored at Cannes and in Spain, and the man inside the institution went on saying what he liked.

Toriyama, by contrast, never said anything. He was unusable in a different way: a recluse with a robot avatar who made one appearance in the United States, in 2002, and would not leave Aichi. A full author who talks too much, and an empty author who will not appear. The state could use neither of them directly. It had to wait.

## 7. Part Five: The Performers (2013–2026)

### A prime minister in a red cap

On August 21, 2016, at the closing ceremony of the Rio Olympics, a green pipe rose out of the stadium floor and Shinzo Abe climbed out of it dressed as Super Mario. The video that preceded it had shown Doraemon, Pac-Man, Hello Kitty and Captain Tsubasa. The Prime Minister of Japan, born in 1954, who had not grown up on any of it, had become the first head of government to physically perform his country's pop-culture IP for a global audience.

This was the discovery that Cool Japan's committees had missed. The state could not originate the characters. But it could *wear* them, for nothing, and the wearing reached more people in eight seconds than the fund reached in a decade. The failed strategy was origination. The strategy that worked was performance.

Everything after follows this discovery.

In April 2018, Shigeru Ishiba, a future prime minister, opened a figure museum in his home prefecture dressed as Majin Buu, the pink villain from *Dragon Ball Z*.

In July 2021, at the delayed Tokyo Olympics, the athletes marched into the opening ceremony to the overture from *Dragon Quest*, the game whose monsters Toriyama drew, and the orchestra went on through *Final Fantasy* and *Monster Hunter*. Macron, in Tokyo for the Games, met Japanese manga artists and posted a signed drawing of Goku that Toriyama had made for him.

In March 2022, on the day before Gabriel Boric's inauguration as President of Chile, Japan's state minister for foreign affairs gave him a plush Squirtle, because Boric had posted that Squirtle was his favorite Pokémon. The Japanese ambassador followed up with two more Pokémon for the interior minister. Someone at the ministry had read the president-elect's social media and chosen the glyph.

### The year the authors left

In 2024 the two engines reached their endpoints within weeks of each other.

Akira Toriyama died on March 1. His studio announced it a week later; the funeral had already happened, privately, with no flowers and no visitors, as he had wished. China's Foreign Ministry sent condolences. Macron posted the signed Goku again, this time to the artist and the millions who grew up with him. Tens of thousands gathered at the obelisk in Buenos Aires and performed the Genki Dama together, arms raised. In May, in Paris, France's prime minister offered Kishida condolences and remarked how many French people knew the series, and Kishida gave him a kokeshi doll carved to look like a *Dragon Ball* character. Two months after the author's death, the state was handing out his shapes.

Two weeks after Toriyama died, *The Boy and the Heron* won Miyazaki his second Oscar. He did not attend; he was eighty-three. In May, Studio Ghibli received an honorary Palme d'Or at Cannes, the first production company ever to receive one, and Goro Miyazaki accepted it. In August, Hayao Miyazaki was named a Ramon Magsaysay laureate, Asia's equivalent of a Nobel, and at the ceremony in Manila he acknowledged the terrible things Japan had done to the Philippines during the war. The same month, a Chinese studio released *Black Myth: Wukong*, a game about the Monkey King who is also Goku, and China claimed the monkey as its own flagship export. One monkey, two states, one year.

Also in 2024, Nippon Television's acquisition of a 42.3 percent stake in Ghibli, completed in October 2023, settled in. Miyazaki was eighty-two and Suzuki seventy-five; the studio had looked for a successor for years, considered Goro, and finally handed management to a television network so the founders could keep drawing. The institution had outgrown the authors. That is what soft power looks like when it matures: a studio that will continue after the man, and a shape that continues after the other man.

### The style without the studio

Then, in March 2025, something happened that no part of this story had prepared for. OpenAI updated ChatGPT's image model, and within days millions of people were turning their photographs into pictures that looked like Studio Ghibli films. Wedding photos, pets, memes, the White House's official account. It was, by any measure, the largest single burst of Ghibli-style imagery in history, and Studio Ghibli had made none of it.

Miyazaki's 2016 reaction to an AI-animated zombie, that it was an insult to life itself, resurfaced and went viral. Goro Miyazaki said the studio's executives were largely indifferent, comparing it to fan art. Suzuki said he had expected people to get bored, and that Miyazaki was not aware of generative AI at all.

Here is what the episode proves. The companion essays argue that the durable asset is never the artifact but the idiom, the visual language others reach for without thinking. In 2025 the Ghibli idiom detached from every artifact and every author and became a filter, applied by strangers to their own lives on an American company's servers. That is soft power at its theoretical limit: the point where the shape no longer needs the studio, the country, or permission. It is also, of course, the point where the owner gets nothing. Toriyama's shapes had reached this point decades earlier, when Mexican city governments screened the *Dragon Ball Super* finale in public squares in 2018 and Toei objected that it was unauthorized. The receiving culture had outrun the owner. Ghibli, the studio that had refused every cut for forty years, arrived at the same place by a different road.

### The palace

Which brings us back to April 1, 2026. Takaichi, born 1961, not a *Dragon Ball* child, initiated the pose. Macron, who was ten when *Club Dorothée* started airing the show in 1988, answered without thinking. Two days later the French Embassy in Tokyo posted its own staff doing it. In June the industry ministry began the process of closing the Cool Japan Fund. In August it was effectively done.

The vehicle that tried to buy soft power died in the same year that soft power reached its most compressed form, a free gesture at a podium. Nobody in Tokyo seems to have noticed the coincidence. They should have.

## 8. How It All Blends: Nine Laws of Japanese Soft Power

### Law 1. It follows the dub, not the alliance

Mark the countries where a head of state has publicly performed or received Japanese pop culture: France, Chile, Argentina, Mexico, Iraq's children in 2004. Now mark the countries whose broadcasters bought Japanese cartoons between 1974 and 1999: *Heidi* across Europe and Latin America, *Dragon Ball* in France from 1988 and Latin America from the mid-1990s, *Captain Tsubasa* in the Arab world. The maps are the same map, and no ministry drew it. Programming executives at TF1, Televisa and Gulf television did, for their own reasons. Soft power flows through channels the receiver cut.

The United States is the exception that proves it. The largest market has produced no head-of-state *Dragon Ball* moment, and it is the one market that re-authored the show, new score, new cuts, new dialogue. Americans received Goku with a different soundtrack. The hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: a re-channeled import carries less of its source.

### Law 2. The thirty-year fuse

Macron was ten in 1988. Boric was ten in 1996. Both became heads of state before forty. The Iraqi teenagers who recognized Tsubasa in 2004 had been ten in the mid-1990s. Soft power of this kind has a fuse of about three decades between a child's first exposure and that child's first signature on a treaty, and the fuse is lit by the broadcaster, not the exporter. The Toonami generation reaches American office in the 2030s. The children who did the Ghibli filter in 2025 reach office around 2055.

And on the Japanese side of every lectern stands someone too old to have lit the fuse. Abe (1954), Kishida (1957), Ishiba (1957), Takaichi (1961): none were *Dragon Ball* children. In every exchange the foreigner is the native speaker and the Japanese leader is performing a fluency acquired for the occasion. Japan learns its own export from the people who imported it.

### Law 3. Two protocols: compression and fidelity

Toriyama built shapes that survive any pipe: re-cut, re-scored, pirated, drawn on a napkin. Ghibli built films that travel whole or not at all. Japan's soft power needs both, and they produce different things. Compression produces reach and gesture: the Kamehameha, the Genki Dama in Buenos Aires, the Slime, the Squirtle. Fidelity produces prestige and institutions: Berlin, the Oscars, the Palme d'Or, the Asturias prize, a museum, a park. A state that has only compression is a meme. A state that has only fidelity is a film festival. Japan, by accident, had both founders switch on in 1984.

### Law 4. Even fidelity needs one empty face

The Slime kept Dragon Quest alive. Totoro kept Ghibli alive. Both are closed contours with minimal features, stable while the cast changes, legible at any size, and both pay the bills for everything richer around them. The companion essays call the first kind the *empty face*: the fewer marks it has, the more people it can be. Goku can be worn by a president. Chihiro cannot. Totoro is the one Ghibli character who can be worn, and he is the one on the plush toys, the asteroid and the park gate.

### Law 5. The state is the latecomer, and its job is to explain, not to make

In 2008 a Google product manager in Japan had to convince headquarters to ship the poop emoji. He did not draw it; he explained, citing Toriyama's *Dr. Slump*, why something that already existed deserved to ship. That is the Japanese state's position toward all of this. It made nothing: not Goku, not Totoro, not Pokémon, not Tsubasa. Its successful acts, Tsubasa on the trucks, Doraemon as ambassador, Mario at Rio, the Squirtle, the kokeshi, the pose, are all explanations of why something the market already chose should be carried further. Its unsuccessful act, the fund, was an attempt to make. The fund is gone. The pose remains.

### Law 6. Honor the object, mute the author

Both authors were unusable. Toriyama would not appear. Miyazaki would not stop talking, and what he said was that the state was wrong about the war. The state's workaround for each was the same: separate the work from the man. Toriyama's shapes became fully available for state use only after March 2024, when the man who might have objected, or simply not shown up, was gone; two of the three official state gestures with his work post-date his death. Miyazaki's films became state assets while the man was named a Person of Cultural Merit and his opinions went unquoted. A dead recluse and a living dissenter are, to a foreign ministry, the same problem, and the solution is the same: wear the object.

### Law 7. Nobody owns the source

Goku is Chinese. Heidi is Swiss. Anne is Canadian. Nausicaä is Greek. The White Snake is from Hangzhou. The first thing that made Miyazaki want to animate was a Japanese imitation of Disney telling a Chinese story. In 2024 China claimed the Monkey King with a video game five months after Japan's Monkey King died. The companion essays' law holds at national scale: the credit goes not to the source but to whoever compressed it best, and Japan's great skill has been refinement of other people's stories into shapes a ten-year-old anywhere can copy. France endorsing a Japanese reduction of a Chinese text at a press conference about Iranian shipping lanes is the loop closing on itself.

### Law 8. The frivolity tax falls on the receiver

When Boric cited *Captain Tsubasa*, *Dragon Ball*, *Saint Seiya* and *Pokémon Yellow* at a Tokyo business forum in May 2025, he was attacked at home for being unserious, and defended himself by posting the Pikachu-shaped dessert the Japanese prime minister had served him and noting that the Japanese are proud of anime as their ambassador. The sender pays nothing; the receiver pays in credibility. Miyazaki pays a version of this too, in reverse: every political statement he makes costs him a little of the apolitical reverence that makes him exportable, and the state quietly absorbs the cost by not repeating what he said.

### Law 9. The endpoint is the style without the country

The 2025 Ghibli filter is the end of the road that the Mexican screenings and the Buenos Aires crowd were on. An idiom so thoroughly established that it can be applied by anyone to anything, with no artifact, no author, no studio and no Japan in the loop. This is the maximum possible reach and the minimum possible control. Kurita, the designer of the first widely copied emoji, warned that modern emoji were simply pictures. Miyazaki called the AI zombie an insult to life. Both were watching the same thing: the moment a compressed shape stops belonging to the people who compressed it. Soft power, pursued far enough, becomes nobody's power.

## 10. What This Teaches

**You cannot buy what the survey chose for free.** The Cool Japan Fund spent 204 billion yen trying to originate attraction and lost 54 billion. A children's ranking in a weekly magazine produced Goku for the price of a page. If you want cultural power, find the number that is already choosing, and ride it.

**Count the fuse.** Thirty years from a child's first exposure to that child's first treaty. Policy made for this year is thirty years late; the programming decision made in Paris in 1988 is the one that paid off at the palace in 2026.

**Keep both protocols.** Compression gives you reach and a gesture a president can make. Fidelity gives you Cannes and a museum. One without the other is either a meme or a film festival.

**Build one empty face, even if you hate empty faces.** The studio that refused every cut was saved by a plush toy. The author who drew every frame is known to most of the world through a round forest spirit with two eyes.

**Honor the object, and let the author say what he likes.** Japan's two greatest cultural assets were a recluse and a dissenter. The state learned to carry their shapes without needing their consent, and it should be honest that this is what it is doing.

**The endpoint is losing control.** Mexican plazas, Buenos Aires crowds, a filter on an American server. The further a shape travels, the less it belongs to whoever made it. That is the price of the reach, and no fund, treaty or policy can collect it back.

**The state is the passer-by.** It did not draw the flyer. It walked past it thirty years later, recognized it instantly, and claimed it. That is what states are for. But the recognition belongs to the children who chose the flyer, the fidelity belongs to the men who refused the cuts, and the power belongs to whoever made the thing recognizable at a glance.
